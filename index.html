Is it worth it?

Should we buy the bond at this price? The stock? Launch the project, buy the investment property, acquire the company? Every decision in this course is the same question. Finance answers it with one equation, and it is worth it when value exceeds the price you pay.

Value starts with what an asset does for you; for financial assets, that is cash. To put a number on it you need four more answers. Pick a piece of the equation to see which lectures supply it, or follow the semester in order from the strip above.

Then ask the question for real

Bonds, stocks and projects are all judged with the same equation. Only the cash flows, horizon and risk change.

Prologue · Finance: the friend we all love to hate

Is finance really the villain?

Politicians, the press and Hollywood love to cast finance as the bad guy. Surveys back it up: in one, 48% of American adults said the financial system hurts the economy and only 34% said it helps. Even 57% of Economist readers doubted that financial innovation boosts growth (Zingales, 2015).

Strip away the caricature and finance is something simpler: the study of the optimal allocation of scarce financial resources. It decides how capital is procured, deployed and distributed. A robust, efficient financial system makes specialization and exchange possible, and that is where progress comes from.

Allocating scarce capital well means asking, again and again, is it worth it?

Lecture 6 · Applying the whole equation

Capital budgeting: is the project worth it?

Is it worth it? A project is worth it when the present value of what it returns exceeds what it costs. That is NPV > 0.
You are hereDeployment→Go/no-go decisions

Go/no-go decisions involve a large initial outlay that creates positive cash flows later. The outlay is the cost and the future cash flows are the benefit, so every go/no-go decision is a valuation exercise. Does the value of the future cash flows exceed the cost?

This page’s running example: the packaging line
Cost today (year 0)$50,000
Cash flow, year 1$15,000
Cash flow, year 2$18,000
Cash flow, year 3$20,000
Cash flow, year 4$12,000
Cash flow, year 5$8,000
Hurdle rate10%

Illustrative. A manufacturer is deciding whether to spend $50,000 on an automated packaging line. Automating cuts labor and wasted materials, and the faster line lets the plant ship more product. The firm expects those savings and extra sales to produce after-tax cash flows of $15,000 in year 1, rising to $20,000 in year 3 as volume ramps up, then falling to $12,000 and $8,000 as the equipment ages and maintenance costs climb. After five years the line is worn out, with no resale value. Every tool on this page is applied to this same project, so you can compare their answers.

Picture it · NPV is a weigh-in

Bring every cash flow back to today, then weigh it against the cost

The packaging line returns $73,000 in total, more than its $50,000 cost. But that $73,000 arrives over five years, and a dollar in year 5 is worth less than a dollar today. NPV puts everything in today’s dollars first. On the right, each year’s cash flow (dashed outline) shrinks to its present value (solid bar). On the left, those present values are stacked up next to the cost. If the stack is taller than the cost, the project adds value; the difference is the NPV.

NPV of the packaging line

Slide the rate up. Later cash flows shrink fastest because they are discounted more times. Somewhere near 15.6% the stack exactly matches the cost: that rate is the IRR.

Picture it · Payback is a climb out of a hole

How long until you are back to zero?

Payback asks a simpler question: how many years until the packaging line has returned its $50,000? Start $50,000 in the hole and add each year’s cash flow. The project pays back partway through the year in which the running total crosses zero.

The Utah Jazz free agent: when IRR gives two answers

From the lecture: you are the GM weighing a power forward who wants a big signing bonus and an eight-year deal. He should fill seats for about three great years, then the contract becomes a drag. The cash flows go negative, then positive, then negative again.

From the lecture: net cash flows ($ millions)
Year 0 (bonus)−10.0
Years 1–2+7.5 each
Year 3+5.5
Years 4–5−0.5 each
Year 6−3.5
Year 7−4.5
Year 8−5.5
Hurdle rate12%

Every time the cash flows switch sign, the NPV curve can turn around, so this curve rises and then falls: it crosses zero twice, at about 12.3% and 35.3%. Both IRRs are above the 12% hurdle, which says “sign him.” But the NPV at 12% is −$41,437. Trust NPV: the contract destroys value.

Modified IRR: force a single answer

Two IRRs happen because the money flows out, in, and out again. MIRR fixes this by rearranging the cash flows so there is one outflow at the start and one inflow at the end, using the hurdle rate (12%) to move them. With only one sign change, there is only one rate.

Combination approach (Excel’s MIRR): every negative cash flow is discounted back to today and every positive one is compounded forward to year 8. Then MIRR = (41.08 ÷ 16.63)1/8 − 1 = 11.965%, below 12%: no go, matching NPV.

The other two approaches move only one side: #1 discounts just the negatives to today; #2 compounds every cash flow after today out to the end. All three land just under 12%.
